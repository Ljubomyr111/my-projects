My Parents, Myself 
Last week I heard the poet Etheridge Knight speak to a group of students and 
teachers. He said there were only two things he knew for certain in his life, and the rest 
was all just his opinion. He said he knew his parents. 
What I have often questioned is whether I am similar to one parent more than 
the other. There were two different people inside my father, or so it seemed to me. My 
early memories of him are positive. He made ships with intricate detail, and he made a 
butterfly collection of specimens he had caught himself. He took us to t he beach. He 
looked self-assured; he looked strong. He was a tall, slender man who had come from 
an English middle -class background, and who  had bettered himself through higher 
education. He met my mother while he was going to law school. She was of pure Irish 
stock, and very much a working-class girl with a little style or class. Once Joe Dimaggio 
had sent her a drink from across the bar, or so she said. Though she was short, she had 
a perfect figure and dressed well on her modest income. She had a pretty smile and 
baby-fine blonde hair which she always wore in a bun on top of her head. My mother 
was without siblings, and her mother passed away when she was 15, leaving her to 
be looked after by her aunt . At the age of twenty-seven, she married my father, 
believing in family and hard work and all the prospects a stable marriage would 
bring. They moved to San Francisco at my father's request and quickly had nine 
children. When I was young, I liked my father more because his attention was more 
difficult to win and therefore more desirable. I remember choosing his home state for 
my sixth-grade report. But, as I got older, I did not want anything to do with him. I 
was no longer as sweet or as pretty, and he was no longer as nice or safe. I didn’t like 
his rules— his careful watching of "form." We were expected to dress before coming 
downstairs in the morning; we were expected to say good night to our father, even if 
he was passed out in his chair. As a teenager, I felt as if I could never do anything 
right. For instance, I could never remember how to set a table properly. More 
importantly, I could not choose the "right" vocation. I dreamed of becoming an artist, 
and he did not have the confidence that I would succeed. 
As a grown-up, I understand why I despaired so much of turning into him . I 

understand his failures because my mother was successful. He didn’t let people into 
his life. He was someone who is always alone, and he became an alcoholic, a word I 
did not know the meaning of until I was twenty-five. I can remember sitting in the half-
lit kitchen waiting for my mom to come home from 
work. Earlier in the evening, my father had come back from Thrifty's as he always 
did on the weekends, with soda and potato chips for us kids, and a bottle for himself. 
He would empty the bag on the counter, putting his beer in the refrigerator, and 
carrying his bourbon to the den. I would sit on the stool and wait to watch his tall 
figure stumble towards the bathroom and complain about this or that. 
People always went closer to my mother. I remember working with her at the 
Pacific Telephone Company and watching the women she worked with be kind and 
polite to her and show her love or care . Some girls called her "Mom," which made 
me feel very sad . Though I was similar to my father , I wanted to feel near my 
mother. When she passed away there were hundreds of people at her funeral. When 
my father died there were only a dozen people – men he had worked with and a few 
people living nearby that had never known him well. 
I remember the Christmas days that followed my mother's death and how my 
father tried to manage. I came to know who he was with some clarity three months 
before he died. I see him lacing red and green ribbons through the chandelier that hung 
over the dining room table and putting out dishes of candy for us kids. I see him 
standing in the living room looking around at us, attempting a few pleasant comments, 
and then retreating awkwardly to his den where he sat with his books and the red 
curtains that hung down from the tall windows, putting coal into the fire because it was 
cheaper than wood. 
My mother was so different. She was honest and didn’t ask for much. She 
offered herself to life. She spent time with the kids at the Sunday school. She let a 
young girl of my own age live with her when that girl's mother could give her neither 
a place to live nor a family. She gave her home to a young man she met in an English 
class who had no goal in life. 

My father didn’t know how to live happily. 
I have often feared not finding my mother's good qualities inside myself. I have 
fought off the separateness my father carried inside himself, his failures, and his 
reserve which people see in me and think silence is peace. I loved them both very 
much.
